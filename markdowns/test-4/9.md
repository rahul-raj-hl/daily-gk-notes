# 📌 India's Trade Policy (1947–1990) & Import Substitution

## ⚡ Quick Summary

- **Core Strategy (1947–1990)**: India's trade policy was primarily **an inward-looking strategy emphasizing import substitution and protection of domestic industries**.
- **Import Substitution Industrialization (ISI)**: Replacing imported goods with domestically manufactured goods to achieve self-reliance (*Atmanirbharta*).
- **Protectionist Instruments**: High customs tariffs, strict import quotas, foreign exchange controls, and stringent import licensing ("License Raj").
- **Public Sector Dominance**: Emphasis on capital-intensive basic and heavy industries under state-led Five-Year Plans (Mahalanobis Model).
- **Shift in 1991**: Transitioned from closed protectionism to an outward-looking economy via **LPG Reforms** (Liberalization, Privatization, and Globalization).

---

## 📖 Main Concept

### 1. Inward-Looking Trade Strategy & ISI (1947–1990)

*The rationale, tools, and philosophy behind post-independence trade policy.*

- **Rationale**:
  - Post-independence leaders feared economic colonization and external dependency.
  - Guided by the *Infant Industry Argument*—protecting nascent domestic industries from foreign competition until they achieved sufficient scale.
- **Key Instruments Used**:
  - **Tariffs**: Imposing steep taxes on imports to make foreign goods uncompetitively expensive.
  - **Quotas & Quantitative Restrictions (QRs)**: Specifying upper ceilings on the physical volume of specific goods that could be imported.
  - **Import Licensing**: Requiring prior government permits and approvals for acquiring raw materials or capital goods from abroad.
  - **Foreign Exchange Control**: Strictly rationing scarce foreign currency reserves via the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA, 1973).

---

### 2. Economic Impact & The 1991 Transition

*From state-directed protectionism to liberalized integration.*

- **Consequences of Pre-1991 Regime**:
  - High industrial diversification and growth of basic core industries (steel, power, engineering).
  - Lack of international competitiveness, poor technological innovation, and monopolies in domestic consumer markets (the "Seller's Market").
  - Severe Balance of Payments (BoP) crisis in 1991 due to low foreign exchange reserves and high fiscal deficits.
- **The Post-1991 Paradigm Shift**:
  - Launch of the **New Economic Policy (NEP) in 1991** under Prime Minister P.V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Dr. Manmohan Singh.
  - Dismantling of industrial licensing, reduction of peak tariffs, devaluation of the rupee, removal of import quotas, and opening of sectors to Foreign Direct Investment (FDI).

## 🧠 Exam Booster

| Feature / Dimension | Pre-1991 Policy (1947–1990) | Post-1991 Policy (Post-Reforms) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Strategy Orientation** | **Inward-Looking (Import Substitution)** | **Outward-Looking (Export Promotion / Globalization)** |
| **Primary Goal** | Self-reliance & Domestic Protection | Global Competitiveness & Trade Integration |
| **Trade Barriers** | Very high tariffs & strict import quotas | Low tariffs & tariffication (elimination of quotas) |
| **Role of Public Sector** | Dominant role (commanding heights of economy) | Market-driven, disinvestment & private participation |
| **FDI Policy** | Heavily restricted and regulated | Liberal automatic approval routes |

## ⚠️ Frequently Confused

| Economic Strategy | Primary Objective |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Import Substitution (Inward-Looking)** | Replace imports with domestic production; protect local industry |
| **Export Promotion (Outward-Looking)** | Produce goods specifically for foreign markets; integrate globally |
| **Protectionism** | Restricting imports via tariffs, quotas, and subsidies |
| **Free Trade / Liberalization** | Unrestricted international exchange of goods with minimal barriers |

During the period of 1947-1990, India's trade policy was largely characterized by:
